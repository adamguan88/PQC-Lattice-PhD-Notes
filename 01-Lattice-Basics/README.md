Lecture 01 — Lattice Basics

1. What Is a Lattice?

A lattice is a discrete set of points in Euclidean space generated by integer combinations of linearly independent vectors.

Let

$$
B = [b_1,b_2,\ldots,b_n]
$$

be a basis consisting of linearly independent vectors.

The lattice generated by $B$ is

$$
\mathcal{L}(B)

\left{
Bz : z\in\mathbb{Z}^n
\right}.
$$

Equivalently,

$$
\mathcal{L}(B)

\left{
z_1b_1+z_2b_2+\cdots+z_nb_n
:
z_i\in\mathbb{Z}
\right}.
$$

The important point is that the coefficients are integers.

---

2. A Simple Example

Consider the basis

$$
b_1=(1,0),
\qquad
b_2=(0,1).
$$

Then

$$
B=
\begin{pmatrix}
1&0\
0&1
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

For

$$
z=
\begin{pmatrix}
z_1\
z_2
\end{pmatrix}
\in\mathbb{Z}^2,
$$

we obtain

$$
Bz

\begin{pmatrix}
z_1\
z_2
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Therefore,

$$
\mathcal L(B)=\mathbb Z^2.
$$

So the ordinary integer grid is itself a lattice.

---

3. Rank and Dimension

For a lattice generated by $n$ linearly independent vectors,

$$
\mathcal L(B)

{Bz:z\in\mathbb Z^n},
$$

the number $n$ is the rank of the lattice.

When the lattice spans an $n$-dimensional Euclidean space, its rank and dimension coincide.

In cryptography, high-dimensional lattices are particularly important because many computational problems on them become difficult as the dimension grows.

---

4. Norms

To discuss short vectors, we need a notion of length.

The Euclidean norm of a vector

$$
x=(x_1,\ldots,x_n)
$$

is

$$
|x|_2

\sqrt{x_1^2+\cdots+x_n^2}.
$$

For example,

$$
x=(3,4)
$$

has

$$
|x|_2=5.
$$

The concept of a "short" lattice vector therefore depends on the norm being used.

---

5. The Shortest Vector Problem

The Shortest Vector Problem (SVP) asks us to find a nonzero lattice vector of minimum length.

Formally,

$$
\lambda_1(\mathcal L)

\min_{v\in\mathcal L\setminus{0}}
|v|_2.
$$

The search version of SVP asks us to find a vector

$$
v^\star\in\mathcal L\setminus{0}
$$

such that

$$
|v^\star|_2

\lambda_1(\mathcal L).
$$

The difficulty of finding short vectors in high-dimensional lattices is one of the foundations underlying lattice-based cryptography.

---

6. The Closest Vector Problem

SVP asks:

«What is the shortest nonzero vector in the lattice?»

The Closest Vector Problem (CVP) asks a different question.

Given a target vector

$$
t\in\mathbb R^n,
$$

find a lattice vector

$$
v\in\mathcal L
$$

that minimizes

$$
|t-v|_2.
$$

Formally,

$$
v^\star

\arg\min_{v\in\mathcal L}
|t-v|_2.
$$

Thus:

$$
\boxed{\text{SVP: find a short lattice vector}}
$$

while

$$
\boxed{\text{CVP: find a lattice vector close to a target}}
$$

---

7. Why Are Lattices Important for Cryptography?

The security of many lattice-based cryptographic constructions is connected to the presumed computational hardness of lattice problems.

The basic conceptual chain is:

$$
\text{Lattice Problems}
\longrightarrow
\text{Hardness Assumptions}
\longrightarrow
\text{Cryptographic Constructions}.
$$

However, modern lattice cryptography is not simply "based on SVP."

Problems such as LWE and SIS provide more structured ways of building cryptographic assumptions from lattice hardness.

This distinction will become important in the next lectures.

---

8. From Lattices to LWE

The Learning With Errors (LWE) problem can be written in its basic form as

$$
b=As+e\pmod q.
$$

Here:

$$
A\in\mathbb Z_q^{m\times n},
$$

$$
s\in\mathbb Z_q^n,
$$

and

$$
e\in\mathbb Z_q^m
$$

is a small error vector.

The presence of the error $e$ is crucial.

Without the error,

$$
b=As\pmod q,
$$

the system is simply a linear algebra problem.

With a carefully chosen error distribution,

$$
b=As+e\pmod q,
$$

the problem becomes computationally difficult under appropriate parameter choices.

We will study this in detail in Lecture 02.

The progression of this course is:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Lattices}
\rightarrow
\text{SVP/CVP}
\rightarrow

\rightarrow
\text{Ring-LWE}
\rightarrow

The deeper question is not merely:

«How do these schemes work?»

«Why should we believe their underlying assumptions are hard?»
